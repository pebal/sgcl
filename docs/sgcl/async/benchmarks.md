# Benchmarks: the async module

The setup, the machine, the environments and how the timers are read are described with [the benchmarks of the engine](../../garbage_collector/benchmarks.md); the numbers here were taken the same way, with `benchmarks/compare.sh` (`CASES=async`, the best of three runs of every case, the machine under its desktop load), from `benchmarks/async/async.cpp`, `benchmarks/go/async` and `benchmarks/java/Async.java`. The channel between threads and between tasks, against Go's channel and Java's queues, is on [the benchmarks of the concurrent module](../concurrent/benchmarks.md); this page is what the rest of the module costs on the scheduler: a hop, a wait, a race. Every case is a loop of one operation in a task on the scheduler's workers (an executor's thread where it says), in a goroutine on Go's runtime, in a virtual thread on Java's scheduler (JDK 26, ZGC); nanoseconds per operation, a dash where the language has no counterpart:

| case | SGCL | Go | Java | what the operation is |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| yield | 28 | 100 | 1339 | `co_await yield()` on a worker: the frame pushed on the worker's ring and popped; `runtime.Gosched()`; `Thread.yield()` in a virtual thread |
| executor yield | 89 | — | — | `co_await yield()` on an [executor](executor.md)'s thread: the executor's queue, a node per push |
| strand round trip | 135 | — | — | `co_await on_workers()` then `co_await on(strand)`: two hops through queues |
| await | 103 | 320 | 3292 | `co_await t` of a task that returns at once: its frame allocated, the task started, its end resuming the awaiter; a goroutine started and its answer received; a virtual thread started and joined |
| spawn | 123 | 322 | 3308 | `co_await spawn(t)` of the same task, one at a time: the await, with the scheduler's queue on the way in; the same in Go and Java |
| when_all | 230 | 300 | 2561 | `when_all(t, t)` of two such tasks, per task; two goroutines and a `WaitGroup`; two virtual threads joined |
| timeout | 508 | 689 | 4003 | `timeout(t, 1h)` of a task that returns at once, per race ([timeout](timeout.md)); a `select` on the goroutine's channel and `time.After(1h)`; `CompletableFuture.orTimeout(1h)` completed by a virtual thread |
| select | 291 | 162 | — | `async_select` of a channel case with an element there and a `timeout(1h)` case: the case served at once, the timer armed and cancelled by the case's end; Go's `select` with `time.After` |
| condition variable | 160 to 590 | 131 | 1228 | a turn handed between two tasks through `condition_variable` and `mutex`, per hand-off; `sync.Cond`; `Condition` under a `ReentrantLock` |
| ping-pong | 160 | 135 | 228 | two tasks over two rendezvous channels, per hop; two goroutines over unbuffered channels; two virtual threads over `SynchronousQueue`s |
| generator | 8.2 | 0.3 | — | an `async_generator`'s value taken with `co_await g.next()`; a range-over-func iterator, which Go compiles into a loop |
| mutex | 11.7 | 7.7 | 14.4 | `co_await m.async_scoped_lock()` and the release, uncontended; `sync.Mutex`; `ReentrantLock` |

What the numbers say. The floor is the scheduler's hop: a yield on a worker is 28 ns, a frame through the worker's own ring, against Go's 100 for `Gosched` and Java's 1339 for a virtual thread's yield through its pool; an executor's yield is 89, through the executor's queue, a `concurrent_queue` with a managed node per push (an intrusive list would take that to the ring's neighbourhood). A task awaited costs 103 ns, its managed frame, its start and the hop of its end, where a goroutine started and answered is 320 and a virtual thread started and joined 3300; spawned and awaited 123 against 322 and 3308; `when_all` of two 230 per task against 300 and 2561. A race against a deadline is 508 ns against Go's 689 and Java's 4003: one object, a continuation that is a call into it at the task's end, a timer that calls into it too, and a compare-exchange between the two (1867 before, when the task ran under a runner task finishing into a slot, and the race was a task of its own selecting between the slot's channel and the timer's). The generator is the cheapest thing on the page, 8 ns per value, because it never goes near a queue: `next()` resumes the generator where the consumer runs and a `co_yield` resumes the consumer where the generator is; Go's 0.3 is not a switch at all, the iterator's body compiled into the caller's loop. The rendezvous between two tasks is 160 ns per hop, near Go's 135 and ahead of Java's 228, and a strand's round trip 138: both were 330 to 346 in the previous version of this table, and the difference is one line in `concurrent_queue`, the queue the channel's lists of waiters and the executors' queues are: its push notified the threads waiting in `pop` whether there were any or not, which in libc++ is a fetch-add and a fence on a contention table the whole process shares, and a wake through the kernel whenever the table's entry happened to have a sleeping worker's word in it; gated on a count of the threads in `pop`, the notify is gone from a path that never blocks there. Where SGCL is still behind Go is the wait that needs more than a hand-off: the condition variable, 160 to 590 ns per hand-off against 131 (two modes, run by run: the pair split over two workers, or both on one, where the notifier takes the lock back before the notified runs), and the select with a channel's case served at once, 291 against 162. A wait on the condition variable allocates a channel of its own and registers on it, the mutex is let go of and taken back through its channel, and the notify goes through the queue of waiters: five allocations and as many registrations per hand-off, (the spurious wakes of the old notify used to split the pair over two workers every time, 311 per hand-off in the previous version of this table, at the price of a worker woken for nothing on every hop); a worker that runs out of work clears a page of its dead stack before looking for more, since the collector's scan sees the words the last task left there ([scheduler](scheduler.md)), some 20 ns on a hop that leaves a worker idle; Go's `sync.Cond` keeps a `sudog` per goroutine and allocates nothing. Java's virtual threads sit between: a `SynchronousQueue` hand-off is 228 ns, its condition 1228, and anything that starts a virtual thread is thousands. The mutexes are level, a compare-exchange each.
